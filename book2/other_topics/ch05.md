# Chapter 5 — Extracting Entities: the method on other topics (moved from the printed book)

#### the analyst: where growth conditions live

the rules extract four fields from 191 papers describing new respiratory species: specimen, temperature, atmosphere and culture medium. From abstracts alone, they find a medium in 7% of papers, a temperature in 25% and an atmosphere in 40%. On the 35 papers with open full text, the same rules find a medium in 83%, a temperature in 94% and an atmosphere in 69%. On those same 35 papers, the abstracts gave a medium for only 1.

The rules did not get better; the text got longer. Growth conditions sit in methods and species descriptions, not abstracts. An LLM asked to extract them from abstracts would face the same empty field, and might fill it with a plausible guess. the analyst validates the full-text extraction on a stratified sample, and labels every abstract-only value "not reported" rather than "none".

#### the analyst: product codes, not words

the analyst extracts the intervention from 125 trials returned by a ClinicalTrials.gov search for phage terms. A rule looking for the word "phage" in titles and intervention names finds 88. Some of the others are real phage products named only by code: SNIPR001, LBP-EC01, TP-122A. the analyst builds a small dictionary of product codes from company pipelines and press releases, and adds it to her extraction. In drug and device fields, a dictionary of codes matters as much as one of words.

#### the analyst: uses AI, or studies it?

the analyst wants to separate papers that *use* a large language model as a research tool from papers that *evaluate* one. A title rule for "using", "with" or "via" followed by ChatGPT or an LLM finds 13% of his 13,872 papers; a rule for words such as "performance", "accuracy" or "evaluating" finds 31%. Many titles fit neither, and some fit both. This is a judgement field, the weakest kind (Section 2), so he labels a sample of 80 by hand before trusting either rule.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst opened each of the 35 open papers in Europe PMC and used the browser's find function to jump to the words "agar", "°C" and "anaerob". For each paper he copied the sentence into his sheet, then wrote the value in its own column: medium, temperature, atmosphere. Thirty-five papers took an afternoon. It was slower than a script, but it gave him something a script cannot: a feeling for how authors write growth conditions, which became the examples in his codebook.

In Google Sheets, he could then pull values from pasted sentences with formulas, for example the first temperature: `=REGEXEXTRACT(D2,"\d{1,2}(?:\.\d)?\s*°\s*C")`.

**The code path.** His rules are short patterns, the same for abstracts and full texts.

```python
import re, requests
RULES = {
  "temperature": r"\d{1,2}(?:\.\d)?\s*(?:-|–|to)?\s*\d{0,2}\s*°\s*C",
  "atmosphere": r"\b(?:strictly anaerob\w*|anaerob\w*|aerob\w*|microaerophil\w*|5\s*% ?CO2)",
  "medium": r"blood agar|columbia|chocolate agar|brain heart infusion|trypticase soy|YCFA|Schaedler",
}
def full_text(pmcid):
    r = requests.get(f"https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/{pmcid}/fullTextXML")
    return re.sub(r"<[^>]+>", " ", r.text) if r.ok else ""
def extract(text):
    return {k: sorted({m.lower() for m in re.findall(p, text, re.I)}) for k, p in RULES.items()}
```

The patterns are simple on purpose. Each one can be read, tested and corrected by a microbiologist who does not code, and every value comes with the sentence it came from.

**Where an LLM helps, and where it does not.** An LLM can turn a long species description into a tidy row faster than rules can, and handles unusual wording better. the analyst uses one on the full texts, with two instructions: copy the exact sentence for every value, and answer "not reported" when the text does not say. He then checks that every sentence really is in the paper, and measures the LLM against his own 35 rows. On abstracts he does not use it at all: there is nothing to extract, and an LLM asked to fill a table will fill it.

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** built her dictionary of product codes in a sheet with three columns: code, company, source (a press release, a pipeline page, a paper). Each code entered the dictionary only with a source. She then searched trial titles and intervention names for the codes as whole words, and added the matches to her phage trials, with a column that says how each trial was found: by word or by code.

**the analyst** wrote his two title rules ("uses AI as a tool", "evaluates AI") as columns in his sheet, and counted the four combinations: one rule, the other, both, neither. The "both" and "neither" rows were where his labels would matter most, so he drew his validation sample from them first.
