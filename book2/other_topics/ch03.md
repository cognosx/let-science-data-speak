#### the analyst: what is one patent?

the patent data came as 11,868 publications. The same European application is published more than once: once as an application (kind A1) and again when granted (kind B1). Grouped by application, they make 7,901 inventions. Counting publications would have inflated every trend by half again. In a worldwide landscape the unit is usually the patent family, which joins the filings for one invention in many countries.

Names need the same care. The applicant CJ CheilJedang appears in two spellings ("CJ Cheiljedang Corporation" and "Cj Cheiljedang Corporation"). Counted apart, the company looks like two smaller applicants; joined, it files the most phage applications in her data (23). Normalise names before you rank anyone.

#### the analyst: on the topic, or only near it?

the search returned 191 species descriptions that mention respiratory words. Only 100 of them (52%) state that the species was isolated from a respiratory sample; the others mention the lung, sputum or cystic fibrosis in passing, for species isolated from blood, the gut or the environment. His screening criterion becomes precise: "isolated from a human respiratory sample", checked in the methods, not the abstract alone.

#### the analyst: research, or talk about research?

In 2023, 39% of the papers on large language models were editorials, comments or letters. He screens them out for his question on how researchers *use* AI, but keeps them in a second set: they show how quickly a field's conversation started, which is itself worth reporting.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the patent export had one row per publication. In a spreadsheet, she sorted by application number and saw the pattern at once: the same application appeared as A1 (application) and B1 (grant), sometimes A2 and B2 as well. She used a pivot table with application number as rows to count applications, not publications: 7,901 instead of 11,868.

For applicant names she used OpenRefine, a free desktop tool for cleaning data. Its "cluster and edit" function groups names that differ only by capitals, punctuation or word order ("CJ Cheiljedang Corporation", "Cj Cheiljedang Corporation"). She read each proposed cluster before merging it, because the same tool also proposes clusters for different organisations with similar names.

**The code path.**

```python
import pandas as pd, re
pubs = pd.read_csv("raw_epo_publications.csv")
apps = pubs.groupby("app").agg(title=("title", "first"), applicants=("applicant",
        lambda s: "; ".join(sorted(set(x for x in s if isinstance(x, str))))))
norm = lambda n: re.sub(r"\s+", " ", re.sub(r"[^a-z ]", "", n.lower())).strip()
names = apps.applicants.str.split("; ").explode()
print(names.nunique(), names.map(norm).nunique())   # distinct names before and after
```

**What the analyst reports.** "7,901 European patent applications (11,868 publications), with applicant names normalised; 315 distinct name strings became 292 applicants." The unit and the cleaning are in the first sentence, so no reader mistakes publications for inventions.

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** added one screening question to his form: "Does the paper state that the species was isolated from a human respiratory sample? Yes / No / Not stated in the abstract." He answered from the abstract first, and opened the methods only for "not stated". A first pass with a text rule finds a stated respiratory source in 100 of 191 papers; he reads the rest, and every excluded paper goes to an "excluded, with reason" sheet, which he keeps.

**the analyst** split his corpus into two sets rather than deleting commentary: research papers for his question on use, commentary for a second question on how the debate grew. To measure his publication-type rule, he screens 200 research records himself and records each disagreement with a short reason.
