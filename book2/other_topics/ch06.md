# Chapter 6 — Topic Maps and Semantic Search: the method on other topics (moved from the printed book)

#### the analyst: the class that was mostly something else

Before mapping topics, the analyst looks at what her patent class contains. IPC A61K35/76 covers viruses and bacteriophages used as active ingredients. Of its 3,744 applications, only 5.7% mention phages in the title; the rest are other viruses used as medicines, and about a third of those titles mention cancer, tumours or viral vectors. A topic map of the whole class would show cancer virotherapy as the "main theme of phage therapy". She maps only the phage applications and treats the class as a search aid, not a topic.

#### the analyst: the cluster from elsewhere

the analyst groups his 191 species descriptions by the source of isolation. Only 52% state a respiratory source. Blood, gut and environmental isolates form their own groups, pulled in because their abstracts mention the lung or cystic fibrosis in passing. As in the map, the clusters show what does not belong, and they go back to screening (Chapter 3).

#### the analyst: the topic that is a conversation

the first map of 13,872 papers on large language models is dominated by evaluation: 31% of titles use words such as "performance", "accuracy" or "evaluating", against 13% that describe using an LLM as a tool. In 2023, 39% of papers were editorials, comments or letters. He maps research papers and commentary separately, and labels each map with what it contains, so that readers do not mistake a lively debate for a body of applied research.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst compared two searches in Espacenet, the free search service of the European Patent Office. The first used the class alone: `ipc=A61K35/76`. The second added a title word: `ipc=A61K35/76 and ti=phage`. Reading 50 titles at random from the class search shows what her counts say: most are not about phages, and many are cancer-killing viruses or gene-delivery vectors. She writes the result in one line of her log: "class alone: mostly not phage; class and word: phage."

She then treated her phage applications as her map, and drew their uses (therapy, food and agriculture, tools, unclear) by filing period in a stacked bar chart, from a pivot table.

**The code path.** Her search ran on the EPO's open linked data, with a query in SPARQL, the query language for linked data. The core of it:

```sparql
PREFIX pat: <http://data.epo.org/linked-data/def/patent/>
SELECT ?pub ?date ?title WHERE {
  ?pub pat:classificationIPCInventive <http://data.epo.org/linked-data/def/ipc/A61K35-76> ;
       pat:publicationDate ?date .
  OPTIONAL { ?pub pat:titleOfInvention ?title . FILTER(lang(?title) = "en") }
}
```

In Python, one line flags phage titles as whole words, so that *macrophage* is not counted: `title.str.upper().str.contains(r"\b(?:BACTERIO)?PHAGES?\b|PHAGEMID|PROPHAGE|ENDOLYSIN")`.

**What the analyst reports.** "Of 3,744 European applications in class A61K35/76, 5.7% mention phages in the title. Our landscape maps the phage applications found by class and title words together." One sentence prevents a whole map of the wrong field.

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** drew a simple bar chart of his 191 papers by the source of isolation stated in the text: respiratory, blood, gut, environment, not stated. The non-respiratory bars were the noise his search had collected; their papers went back to screening with a reason.

**the analyst** decides how to label his map before he draws it. His corpus comes from Europe PMC, so it is mostly biomedicine, and 31% of titles are about evaluating AI. Whatever the clusters show, the map will be titled "papers about LLMs in biomedicine, 2023–2025", not "uses of AI in research". He reads ten titles per cluster before naming any of them.
