#### the analyst: do papers on AI get cited more?

the analyst compares citations of papers on large language models with all other papers in the same journals and year. Pooled across nine journals in 2023, the median research paper on LLMs had 41 citations, against 10 for other papers. Within each journal the gap remains: LLM papers had the higher median in eight of nine journals (one journal had no LLM research paper to compare).

the analyst writes this carefully. It is an **association**, not an effect of using AI. Papers on a new, much-discussed topic attract citations, whatever their method, and we did not check how quickly journals handled them. His sentence: "In 2023, papers on large language models were cited more than other papers in the same journals." He does not write that using AI makes a paper more cited, and he adds the distribution: across all 2023 LLM papers, the mean was 43.7 citations, the median 13, and the top 1% of papers held 28% of all citations.

#### the analyst: counting patents over time

#### the analyst: a rate, not a count

the analyst finds 40 new respiratory species descriptions in 2015–2019 and 79 in 2020–2026. Before writing that discovery is accelerating, he asks what the denominator is: the number of all new species described in the same years. If all of taxonomy grew as fast, respiratory discovery did not speed up; it kept pace. He reports the share, with an interval, not the count alone.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst opened the companion file with every 2023 paper from nine journals, each with its citation count from Europe PMC and a column saying whether it is a paper on large language models. He kept research papers only, then built a pivot table: rows are journals, columns are LLM paper or not, and the value is the **median** of citations, not the average. A second pivot table counted papers in each cell, so that he could see where the comparison rested on very few papers.

**The code path.**

```python
r = df[df.research]                         # same journals, same year, research papers only
by_journal = r.groupby(["journal", "llm"]).cites.median().unstack()
by_journal["n_llm"] = r.groupby("journal").llm.sum()
print(by_journal)
print((by_journal[True] > by_journal[False]).sum(), "of", by_journal[True].notna().sum())
```

**What the analyst reports.** "Within the same journals in 2023, research papers on large language models had higher median citations than other papers in 8 of 9 journals (one journal had none to compare)." Then the limit: "This is an association with a new, widely discussed topic. It does not show that using AI makes research more cited." And then the distribution: mean 43.7, median 13, top 1% holding 28% of citations.
