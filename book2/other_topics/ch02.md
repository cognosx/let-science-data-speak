# Chapter 2 — From Question to Corpus: Searching Several Databases: the method on other topics (moved from the printed book)

Every topic hides a search trap. Here are three, found on 10 November 2026, and then your turn.

#### the analyst: the words that disappear

the analyst wants every paper that describes a new bacterial species from a human respiratory sample. Species descriptions carry "sp. nov." in their titles, so he searches PubMed titles for "sp. nov.". He gets 181 records, from all of microbiology, ever. That is impossible: one taxonomy journal, *International Journal of Systematic and Evolutionary Microbiology*, holds over 14,000 records in PubMed.

The search engine treated the abbreviation badly. Searching the single word *nov* in titles found 12,687 records from that journal alone. Europe PMC, searching the same phrase "sp. nov." in titles, found 25,177 records. the final PubMed search combined *nov* in the title with respiratory words in the title or abstract, and then filtered the titles with a pattern for "sp. nov." and "gen. nov.". It returned 193 records, 191 of them true species descriptions.

**Lesson:** test every abbreviation and phrase on its own before you trust it inside a long search. If a count looks too small, check it against a source you know.

#### the analyst: the heading that arrived late

the analyst counts papers on phage therapy by year. The MeSH heading "Phage Therapy" is precise, so she uses it. For 2025 it finds 440 papers; the same concept in titles and abstracts finds 955. For 2015 the heading finds 3 papers, the words 100. Headings are added by indexers after publication, and new headings start late. A trend built on the heading alone would show phage therapy arriving years after it did. the analyst searches both, and reports the words-only count as the main series.

#### the analyst: when the source is closed for the day

the analyst planned to use OpenAlex for its citation counts. On the run date, every request without a key was refused: the free allowance shared by everyone without a key had been used up. He switched to Europe PMC, which also gives citation counts for its records, and logged the change. His corpus is now limited to records Europe PMC indexes, mostly biomedicine, and his log says so. **Access is part of the method**, and it changes what you can find.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst worked in the PubMed web interface, one line at a time, and wrote each count in a search log.

1. `"sp. nov."[ti]`: **181** results. Far too few: test the phrase.
2. `nov[ti] AND "Int J Syst Evol Microbiol"[ta]`: **12,687** results. The word works where the phrase fails.
3. `nov[ti] AND (sputum[tiab] OR respiratory[tiab] OR bronchoalveolar[tiab] OR nasopharyn*[tiab] OR oropharyn*[tiab] OR "cystic fibrosis"[tiab] OR bronchiectasis[tiab] OR "lower respiratory"[tiab]) AND (human[tiab] OR patient*[tiab])`: **193** results. The working search.

He sent the 193 records to a CSV file ("Send to", then "File", then "CSV"), opened it in a spreadsheet, and added a column that flags titles with "sp. nov.", "gen. nov." or "subsp. nov.": 191 of 193. The other two were about a protein named NOV and a renamed subspecies.

He then runs the same concepts in Europe PMC's advanced search, which handles the phrase "sp. nov." in titles, so that he can compare the two lists by PMID.

**The code path.**

```python
from Bio import Entrez
Entrez.email = "you@example.org"
count = lambda q: int(Entrez.read(Entrez.esearch(db="pubmed", term=q, retmax=0))["Count"])
for q in ['"sp. nov."[ti]', 'nov[ti] AND "Int J Syst Evol Microbiol"[ta]']:
    print(count(q), q)
```

Two lines of output told him what an hour of reading would have: the phrase search was broken, the word search was not.

**What the log shows.** The log is the method section. Each line has the database, the date, the exact search and the count, so a reviewer, or the analyst a year later, can rerun it and see what changed.

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** ran her phage therapy concept twice in PubMed for each year from 2000: once with the MeSH heading, once with title and abstract words. She pasted both series into a sheet and drew them on one chart. The heading line starts late and stays below the word line, which is the picture of indexing lag. Her report uses the word series and shows the heading series beside it.

**the analyst** kept a column in his search log for the access route: "web interface", "API without key", "API with key". When OpenAlex refused his requests, the log already had a place to say so. He added the error message and the date, and moved to Europe PMC.
