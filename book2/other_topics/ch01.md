# Chapter 1 — AI You Can Trust: The Validation Recipe: the method on other topics (moved from the printed book)

The recipe works the same way on any topic. Here is how it looks for three researchers, and then for you. Their counts come from simple rules run on real data on 10 November 2026; each one names the check that would turn it into a validated number.

#### the analyst: when the corpus changes under your sample

the analyst collected 13,872 papers on large language models published from 2023 to 2025 (Europe PMC). He wants to know how many report *using* AI in research, rather than discussing it. His first rule is simple: drop editorials, comments and letters.

Before validating, he counts the categories by year. Editorials, comments and letters were 39% of the 2023 papers, 20% of 2024 and 8% of 2025. A field's first year is full of opinion; research follows later. A validation sample drawn mostly from 2025, the largest year, would say little about 2023. **the analyst stratifies by year**: 70 records from each year, labelled by two people into "used AI as a method", "about AI" and "off topic". He reports the accuracy of his rule for each year, not one number for all three.

#### the analyst: a field that is almost always empty

the rule extracts the culture medium from abstracts of papers describing new bacterial species. It finds a medium in only 7% of 191 abstracts. A random sample of 100 abstracts would hold about seven positives: too few to measure precision. the analyst draws 40 abstracts where the rule found a medium and 40 where it found none, and labels both. The second group tells him whether the rule is missing media, or whether the abstracts simply do not report them (Chapter 5 shows it is mostly the second).

#### the analyst: "unclear" is a category

the analyst classifies phage patents by use, from their titles: therapy, food and agriculture, or laboratory tools. Her rules leave 170 of 381 phage applications (45%) unclassified. Her first codebook had no place for these, so her colleague forced each one into a category and the two disagreed constantly. the analyst adds **"unclear from the title"** to the codebook, with the rule "read the first claim before deciding". Agreement rises, and "unclear" becomes a finding: titles alone cannot tell you what a patent is for.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst exported his records from Europe PMC as a spreadsheet, with title, journal, year and publication type. He added three columns:

1. **kind**, from publication type: "editorial, comment or letter", "review", or "research or other". In Google Sheets, `=IF(REGEXMATCH(LOWER(F2),"editorial|comment|letter|news"),"commentary",IF(REGEXMATCH(LOWER(F2),"review"),"review","research or other"))`.
2. **random**, with `=RAND()`, then copied and pasted as values so the sample does not change.
3. **stratum**, the year.

He sorted by year, then by the random column, and took the first 70 rows of each year: 210 records. He and a colleague then label them separately, in two copies of the sheet, as "used AI as a method", "about AI" or "off topic", and compare. Where they disagree, they read the abstract together and note why in a fourth column. The notes become his codebook's examples.

To measure his rule, he makes a two-by-two table for each year: rule said "research" or not, people said "used AI as a method" or not. From the four cells he computes precision and recall, and uses the Wilson formula from Section 3 for the intervals.

**The code path.** The same steps take a few lines.

```python
import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_csv("llm_papers_2023_2025.csv")
pt = df.pubType.fillna("").str.lower()
df["kind"] = "research or other"
df.loc[pt.str.contains("editorial|comment|letter|news"), "kind"] = "commentary"
df.loc[pt.str.contains("review") & (df.kind != "commentary"), "kind"] = "review"
print(df.groupby("pubYear").kind.value_counts(normalize=True).unstack().round(2))

sample = df.groupby("pubYear", group_keys=False).sample(70, random_state=1)
sample.assign(label_1="", label_2="", note="").to_csv("to_label.csv", index=False)
```

After labelling, `statsmodels.stats.proportion.proportion_confint(k, n, method="wilson")` gives each interval.

**What the analyst reports.** One line per year, never one number for all: "In 2023, the rule kept X of Y research papers that used AI (recall, 95% CI) …". Because the share of commentary fell from 39% to 8% over three years, a single number would have hidden a rule that worked well in 2025 and poorly in 2023.

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** filtered his sheet to the rows where the rule found a medium and drew 40 at random, then did the same for the rows where it found none. For each, he wrote the medium he could see in the abstract, or "not reported". In the second group, most abstracts truly named no medium: the rule was not missing much, the abstracts were empty. That finding sent him to the full texts (Chapter 5).

**the analyst** added "unclear from the title" to her codebook before labelling, with one rule: read the first claim, and only then decide. Reading a claim takes a minute or two, so the 170 unclear applications are a few hours of work: affordable, and far cheaper than a landscape built on guesses.
