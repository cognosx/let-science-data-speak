#### the analyst: who files with whom

the analyst builds a network of co-applicants from her 381 phage patent applications. Only 102 applications (27%) name two or more applicants, giving 188 links. The strongest link joins two Portuguese companies that file together eight times; the next joins two institutes of the Polish Academy of Sciences, four times. Before she draws the network, she joins name variants (Chapter 3) and reads the 20 largest nodes, because one company filed under two spellings. A network of patents shows formal partnerships only: licences, acquisitions and informal collaboration leave no trace in applicant fields.

#### the analyst: a knowledge graph with its evidence

the analyst turns his extracted data into a small knowledge graph: *species — isolated from — specimen* and *species — grown on — medium*. The rule he sets is strict: no edge without the sentence it came from. On his 35 open full texts, the rules found a medium for 29 papers; each edge carries its paper ID and the sentence. When a medium appears only in a reference list or a comparison with other species, he deletes the edge. A graph without its evidence would turn every mention into a fact.

#### the analyst: a network that measures attention, not influence

the analyst considers a citation network of papers on large language models. Most of his papers are less than three years old, and in 2023 a few early papers collected a large share of citations. A network built now would show which papers were cited first, not which ones shaped the field. He decides to wait, and to compare two snapshots a year apart before drawing any conclusion about influence.

#### How the analyst did it

**The no-code path.** the analyst filtered her sheet to applications with two or more applicants: 102 of 381. For each one she wrote the pairs of applicants in an edge list, one pair per row, with the application number. A pivot table counted how often each pair appeared. She opened the edge list in Gephi, a free tool for drawing networks, and coloured nodes by applicant type (company, public, individual). Two groups stood out: a pair of Portuguese companies that file together often, and a cluster of Polish Academy of Sciences institutes.

**The code path.**

```python
import itertools, networkx as nx
G = nx.Graph()
for names in apps.applicant_list:            # one list of normalised names per application
    for a, b in itertools.combinations(sorted(set(names)), 2):
        w = G.get_edge_data(a, b, {"weight": 0})["weight"]
        G.add_edge(a, b, weight=w + 1)
print(sorted(G.edges(data="weight"), key=lambda e: -e[2])[:5])
```

**What the analyst reports.** She reports the strongest partnerships with their application counts, and one limit in plain words: "Applicant fields show formal co-filing only; licences, acquisitions and informal collaborations are not visible."

#### How the analyst and the analyst did it

**the analyst** stored his graph as a table with five columns: species, relation, value, paper ID, and the sentence. Anyone can open it in a spreadsheet, and every edge can be checked in seconds. Before sharing it, he reads a random 10% of the edges against the papers.

**the analyst** wrote down the rule that will decide when his citation network is ready to read: two snapshots a year apart, and a conclusion only about papers whose position is stable in both.
